Chapter 7 · the mail route

Mail that flies and people who move house

Wireless networks are like a mail carrier who delivers by shouting across the street. Mobility is what happens when the person you are writing to keeps moving house. Follow the route, try every lab, and collect a stamp at each stop.

Packet = letter Base station = local post office Home network = your home address Tunnel = envelope inside an envelope

    STOP01

    Why wireless and mobile?

    Two different problems hiding in one chapter title.

    In 2019 there were about 10 mobile phone subscribers for every 1 fixed phone line, and 5 mobile-broadband devices for every 1 fixed-broadband device. 4G and 5G networks now run the same Internet protocol stack you already know, including SDN.

    The chapter splits into two separate challenges:

    Challenge 1Wireless

    Sending letters through the air, where they fade, bounce and get drowned out by noise.

    Challenge 2Mobility

    Delivering to someone who changes their point of attachment, like a friend who keeps moving house.

    MAIL

    Wireless does not always mean mobile. A smart TV on Wi-Fi never moves, just like a house that gets its letters by carrier pigeon but never changes address.

    STOP03

    Why air mail is harder than a wire

    Fading, multipath, noise and hidden terminals.

    FadingPath loss

    The signal loses power as it travels, even in free space. A shout gets quieter the further it goes.

    EchoesMultipath

    Signals bounce off ground and buildings, so copies arrive at slightly different times. Coherence time is how long a bit stays readable; it shrinks as frequency and receiver speed go up, which caps the rate.

    NoiseInterference

    Motors and appliances use the same frequencies. A bigger SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) makes the letter easier to read.

    STOP04

    CDMA: everyone talks, each in their own code

    Code Division Multiple Access.

    All users share the same frequency at the same time. Each one gets a unique chipping sequence (code). If the codes are orthogonal, receivers can pull one sender's bits out of the mixed-up channel.

    encode: Zi,m = di · cm    decode: di = (1/M) Σ Zi,m · cm

    MAIL

    STOP05

    Wi-Fi: the 802.11 post office

    Architecture, joining, CSMA/CA, addressing and power saving.

    The branchBSS (a "cell")

    A Basic Service Set holds wireless hosts plus one access point (AP). Ad hoc mode has hosts only.

    Which counterChannels

    The spectrum is split into channels. The AP admin picks one, and a neighbour's AP might pick the same one and interfere.

    Signing upAssociation

    Scan channels for beacon frames with the AP's name (SSID) and MAC, pick an AP, maybe authenticate (Chapter 8), then run DHCP for an IP address.

    PASSIVE

    Passive scanning: post offices hang signs (beacons). You read the signs, then send an association request and get a response.

    ACTIVE

    Active scanning: you shout "any post offices here?" (probe request). Each AP answers with a probe response, then you associate.

    Multiple access: avoid crashes, because you cannot see them

    Wi-Fi uses CSMA: listen before you talk. But it has no collision detection. While transmitting, your own loud signal drowns out the weak incoming one, and hidden terminals and fading hide collisions anyway. So 802.11 aims to avoid them: CSMA/CA.

    Lab

    Step through CSMA/CA

    RTS/CTS

    For big parcels, the sender first sends a tiny Request-To-Send to the AP. The AP announces Clear-To-Send to everyone, like a clerk calling "counter reserved for A!". All other stations wait. Short RTS notes may still collide, but they are cheap to resend.

    STOP06

    Bluetooth: the tiny neighbourhood (PAN)

    Personal area networks under 10 m across.

    PurposeCable replacement

    Mouse, keyboard, headphones. Ad hoc, no infrastructure. 2.4–2.5 GHz ISM band, up to 3 Mbps.

    PiconetMaster and clients

    The master polls clients and grants their requests to transmit. Clients can park (sleep) to save battery. Nodes self-assemble, plug and play.

    HoppingTDM + FDM

    625 µs time slots. The sender hops over 79 channels in a known pseudo-random order, so outside interference only hits some slots.

    STOP07

    4G LTE: the national postal service

    Wide-area mobile Internet, standardized by 3GPP.

    Like the wired Internet

    Edge and core (both owned by the carrier), a network of networks, and the protocols you know: HTTP, DNS, TCP, UDP, IP, NAT, SDN, Ethernet, tunneling.

    Different from it

    A different wireless link layer, mobility as a first-class service, user identity on a SIM card, and a business model built on subscribers who roam on other carriers.

    Sharing the radio: OFDM

    Each device connects to an eNode-B. There are many bands (600, 700, 850, 1500, 1700, 1900, 2100, 2600, 3500 MHz) with separate upstream and downstream channels. The radio is shared with OFDM, a mix of frequency and time division, giving hundreds of Mbps per device.

    Lab

    Tunneling: an envelope inside an envelope

    The phone's datagram is wrapped with the GPRS Tunneling Protocol (GTP), inside UDP, inside an IP datagram from base station to S-GW. When the phone moves, only the tunnel endpoints change.

    GLOBAL

    The global cellular network is a network of IP networks. Your home carrier and the visited carrier meet at exchange points (the public Internet and the inter-carrier IPX), like national post services swapping sacks at the border. Legacy 2G/3G were not all-IP.

    STOP08

    5G: faster vans, more vans, tiny branches

    Goals over 4G.

    10×peak bitrate
    10×lower latency
    100×traffic capacity
    eMBBEnhanced Mobile Broadband

    Huge parcels, fast. Video and big downloads. Most early 5G launches focused here.

    URLLCUltra-Reliable Low Latency

    Express courier that must never be late: factories, vehicles, remote health.

    mMTCMassive Machine-Type

    Millions of tiny postcards from sensors and meters.

    5G NR (New Radio) uses two bands: FR1 (450 MHz–6 GHz) and FR2 (24–52 GHz, millimeter wave). It is not backward compatible with 4G. Millimeter waves carry much more data but over shorter distances, so 5G needs pico-cells 10–100 m across and a massive, dense rollout of new base stations. MIMO uses many directional antennas.

    The 5G core is SDN-like: separate control plane (resource control) and user plane, spread across edge, far-edge and core data centers. What comes next is more often called "NextG" or "Beyond 5G": it evolves continuously like the Internet, and enterprises can run their own Private 5G without waiting for standards.

    STOP09

    Mobility: your friend moved house

    How does the network find a device that changes networks?

    Mobility is a spectrum. At one end you move inside a single Wi-Fi network (no big deal). At the other you move between networks while keeping a call or TCP connection alive (hard). There are two approaches:

    Option ALet routers handle it

    Routers advertise the mobile's permanent address through normal routing. Longest prefix match would just work, but it does not scale to billions of devices.

    Option BLet end systems handle it

    Functionality at the edge: indirect routing (via the home network) or direct routing (correspondent gets the foreign address and sends there).

    HOME

    To find a friend who moves a lot, you do not search every phone book. You call their parents. A home network is that definitive place that always knows where you are. In 4G/5G the home carrier's HSS plays the parents. Wi-Fi and ISPs have no global "home" (eduroam is an exception).

    STOP10

    Mobility in practice: 4G

    Four jobs the network does when you roam.

    Job 1Base station association

    The mobile sends its IMSI (who am I, who is my home) to the visited base station.

    Job 2Control-plane setup

    The visited MME uses the IMSI to contact the home HSS for authentication, encryption and service info. Now home knows where you are.

    Job 3Data-plane tunnels

    BS ↔ S-GW tunnel (change one endpoint when you switch BS), and S-GW ↔ home P-GW tunnel (that is indirect routing).

    Job 4Handover

    Switch base stations mid-conversation without dropping letters.

    Lab

    Handover, one step at a time

    Mobile IP · RFC 5944The idea that came too early

    Standardized about 20 years ago, before smartphones. Indirect routing with tunnels. The home agent = 4G HSS + home P-GW. The foreign agent = 4G MME + S-GW. Never widely deployed: Wi-Fi for data and 2G/3G for voice were "good enough".

    Upper layersDoes TCP care?

    Logically, no: best effort stays the same and TCP/UDP run fine. Performance-wise, yes: bit errors and handovers lose packets, and TCP thinks every loss is congestion. Real-time traffic suffers from delay, and wireless bandwidth is scarce.

    STOP12

    End of the route

    Chapter 7 on one postcard.

    Wireless

    Links fade, echo and pick up noise. Hidden terminals stop collision detection, so Wi-Fi avoids collisions with CSMA/CA, ACKs and RTS/CTS. CDMA lets users share via codes. Bluetooth hops around 79 channels. 4G uses OFDM resource blocks; 5G adds mmWave, MIMO and pico-cells.

    Mobility

    A home network keeps track of you. Indirect routing forwards via home (transparent, triangle routing). Direct routing uses a care-of address. 4G does association, MME + HSS setup, GTP tunnels and handovers. Mobile IP had the same ideas but never took off. TCP misreads wireless loss as congestion.
