Chapter 4 · Network Layer: Data Plane

One letter, all the way to Bob

Alice wants to send Bob a letter across the city. Penny the mail carrier will follow it through every sorting office on the way. Each scene starts with a puzzle. Make a guess, then watch what really happens.

Scene 1 · slides 4–8

Two different jobs

Alice writes 223.1.2.9 (Bob's address) on the envelope and drops it in the postbox. Between her house and Bob's there are four sorting offices, and at every one somebody has to decide which door the letter leaves by.

Does each sorting office need to know the whole route to Bob?

Think of a road trip to a town you've never been to. You don't carry the whole map in your head. At each junction you read the road sign: "Bobtown → turn left". A router does exactly that. Its forwarding table is the set of road signs at that one junction.

Penny's notebook

  • Forwarding: move a letter from an office's input door to the right output door. Local, done in hardware, takes nanoseconds. This is the data plane.
  • Routing: work out the whole path from source to destination. Network-wide, done in software, takes milliseconds. This is the control plane.
  • The network layer wraps the transport segment in a datagram at the sender and unwraps it at the receiver. Every host and router speaks it.

Those charts all agreed with each other. So who writes them?

What does the post office promise?

Alice is excited, so she writes Bob five letters, numbers them 1 to 5, and posts them all at once.

What will Bob find in his mailbox?

The Internet's network layer is best effort. It tries hard, but it promises nothing: no guaranteed delivery, no order, no minimum bandwidth, no timing. If Alice needs a receipt, a layer above (like TCP) has to arrange one.

A post office that promises nothing... and yet the whole world uses it. Why?

Penny's notebook

  • The mechanism is simple, so it could be deployed everywhere.
  • With enough bandwidth, voice and video are "good enough" most of the time.
  • Copies of services (data centres, CDNs) sit close to users.
  • Congestion control helps "elastic" services cope.
Scene 3 · slides 14–29

Inside the sorting office

Penny follows Alice's letter through the front door of Office 1. Inside is a huge building, yet the letter is out the other side almost instantly.

What happens to the letter in those few nanoseconds?

Rush hour: three letters arrive at three doors at the same moment. How does the building carry them all across?

Penny's notebook

  • A router = input ports + switching fabric + output ports, with a routing processor on top.
  • Lookup happens at the input port, using its own copy of the table ("match plus action").
  • Destination-based forwarding looks only at the destination IP. Generalized forwarding can look at any header field (Scene 12).
  • Switching rate: with N inputs, the fabric should run about N times the line rate.
Scene 4 · slides 17–23

Two lines match!

Back at Office 1's input door, a letter for 200.23.24.170 arrives. The clerk checks the chart. It doesn't list single addresses, only address prefixes (the first bits). Penny reads the address in binary and gets confused.

The green bits match on two lines. Which door should the letter go through?

This rule is longest prefix matching. When several entries fit, use the one with the longest matching prefix, because it is the most specific. It's like a chart saying "postcodes starting SW go to bag 3" and "SW1A 1AA goes to bag 1".

Penny's notebook

  • Forwarding tables store prefixes, not every address.
  • Longest prefix wins.
  • Routers do this in one clock cycle with TCAMs (ternary content-addressable memory). A Cisco Catalyst holds about 1 million entries.
Scene 5 · slides 30–41

Rush hour

It's Monday morning and the whole city is posting letters. At Office 1's exit, letters pour in 3 per tick, but the road out can only take 1 per tick. The mailbag at the door holds 5 letters.

What happens after a few ticks?

Penny's notebook

  • Output queueing: when letters arrive faster than the link can send, they wait (delay) and, when the buffer is full, get dropped (loss).
  • Drop policy: tail drop (drop the newcomer), or drop by priority. Routers can also mark letters to warn of congestion (ECN, RED).
  • How big a buffer? RFC 3439: RTT × link capacity (250 ms × 10 Gbps = 2.5 Gbit). With N flows: RTT·C/√N. Too much buffering causes long delays ("bufferbloat").

There's a sneakier jam at the input doors. Can a letter get stuck even when its exit is empty?

The road is free again. Nine letters are waiting: some Priority, some Standard, some Bulk. Which one goes next?

Who decides the rules? Network neutrality

Scheduling and buffer rules are exactly what an ISP could use to favour some traffic. The 2015 US FCC Open Internet order set three bright lines: no blocking, no throttling, no paid prioritization. The legal question is whether an ISP is a "telecommunications service" (Title II, common-carrier duties) or an "information service" (Title I).

Two offices have a mistake in their charts and keep passing a letter back and forth. Does it bounce forever?

Penny's notebook

  • TTL goes down by 1 at every router. At 0 the datagram is dropped, so loops can't last forever.
  • The upper layer field says who opens it at the end (6 = TCP, 17 = UDP).
  • Overhead: 20 bytes TCP + 20 bytes IP = 40 bytes per segment, before any app data.
  • Routers also send error notes using ICMP.
Scene 7 · slides 45–51

Streets and neighbourhoods

Alice lives at 223.1.1.1. Her street has two other houses: 223.1.1.2 and 223.1.1.3. Bob's cousin lives at 223.1.2.2, over on another street.

Can Alice hand a letter straight to 223.1.1.3 without the sorting office? What about 223.1.2.2?

Penny's notebook

  • An IP address is a 32-bit number for an interface (where a device meets a link), not for the device. Routers have many; laptops have one or two.
  • 223.1.1.1 = 11011111 00000001 00000001 00000001
  • A subnet = interfaces that can reach each other without a router. Same subnet part (high bits), different host part (low bits).
  • /24 is the subnet mask: the first 24 bits are the subnet part.

Does the street part always have to be 24 bits?

No. CIDR (Classless InterDomain Routing, said "cider") lets the subnet part be any length. Write it as a.b.c.d/x, where x is the number of subnet bits.

Scene 8 · slides 52–58

The house with no address

Alice buys a new laptop and opens it in a café. It has no IP address at all. It can't sign a return address, and it doesn't even know who to ask.

How can it send a letter asking for an address, when it doesn't have one?

Penny's notebook

  • DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol) hands out addresses automatically: plug-and-play. Addresses are leased, renewable, and reused when you leave.
  • Four messages: Discover, Offer, Request, ACK ("DORA"). All broadcast, since the client has no address yet.
  • Runs over UDP (server port 67, client 68), inside IP, inside an Ethernet frame sent to FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF.
  • Also returns: first-hop router, DNS server, network mask. Often the DHCP server is built into the router.
Scene 9 · slides 59–63

Who owns the street names?

Alice works at Organization 1. Its addresses, 200.23.18.0/23, came from its ISP, Fly-By-Night, which owns the bigger block 200.23.16.0/20 and sliced it into eight pieces for eight customers.

WhoBinaryBlock

Organization 1 switches to a different ISP, ISPs-R-Us, but keeps its addresses. Fly-By-Night still tells the world "send me everything in 200.23.16.0/20". Where does a letter for Organization 1 go now?

Penny's notebook

  • Hierarchical addressing lets one ISP advertise one block for many customers: route aggregation.
  • A more specific route plus longest prefix match handles customers who move.
  • ISPs get blocks from ICANN via 5 regional registries. ICANN also manages the DNS root. The last IPv4 chunks went out in 2011.
Scene 10 · slides 65–69

A whole house, one address

At home, Alice has a laptop and a phone. But the ISP gave the whole house just one address: 138.76.29.7. Inside, the devices use private addresses like 10.0.0.1 that mean nothing to the outside world.

The laptop asks a web server for a page. How does the reply find the laptop and not the phone?

Penny's notebook

  • NAT: all devices on a local network share one public IPv4 address. Private ranges: 10/8, 172.16/12, 192.168/16.
  • Outgoing: replace (source IP, port) with (NAT IP, new port) and remember it. Incoming: look it up and swap back.
  • 16-bit port numbers allow about 60,000 connections on one address.

It works. But is NAT a good idea?

For it

  • One address from the ISP for every device.
  • Change home addresses without telling anyone.
  • Change ISP without renumbering.
  • Inside devices can't be addressed directly from outside.

Against it

  • Routers "should" only process up to layer 3, but NAT rewrites ports.
  • The address shortage "should" be solved by IPv6.
  • It breaks the end-to-end argument.
  • NAT traversal: outsiders struggle to reach a server behind NAT.

Either way, NAT is here to stay: homes, institutions, and 4G/5G cellular networks all use it.

Scene 11 · slides 70–77

The new envelope

32-bit addresses are running out, so a new envelope was designed: IPv6, with 128-bit addresses and a fixed 40-byte header. Alice's office (A, B) and Bob's office (E, F) have upgraded. But offices C and D in the middle of town still only read old IPv4 envelopes.

How can an IPv6 letter get through C and D?

Penny's notebook

  • Tunneling: carry an IPv6 datagram as the payload of an IPv4 datagram ("a packet within a packet"). Also used in 4G/5G.
  • IPv6 drops the checksum, fragmentation in routers, and options (moved to "next header"). It adds a flow label.
  • Adoption: about 40% of Google's clients use IPv6 (2023). It has taken 25+ years, because the network layer is hard to change.

Scene 13 · slides 79–88

The new rule book

Office 1 gets a new manager, who hands the clerks a rule book. The rules don't just read the "To" address. They can look at anything on the envelope, and do more than forward: drop it, change it, or ask head office.

Rule book (highest priority first)
MatchActionCount

A letter from 128.119.1.1 going to 51.6.0.8 arrives. Two rules match. What happens?

Penny's notebook

  • Generalized forwarding = match + action. Match on link, network or transport fields. Actions: forward, drop, modify, send to controller. Priority breaks ties; counters track bytes and packets.
  • OpenFlow is the standard way to write these flow tables.
  • One idea covers many devices: router (longest IP prefix → forward), switch (MAC → forward or flood), firewall (IP and ports → permit or deny), NAT (IP and port → rewrite).
  • Program the tables across many switches and you get network-wide behaviour.

So where should the cleverness live: inside the network, or at Alice's and Bob's houses?

The IP hourglass

Lots of apps on top (HTTP, SMTP, RTP…), lots of link types underneath (Ethernet, Wi-Fi, cable…), and one thin waist in the middle: IP. In "middle age" the waist has thickened a little, with NAT, firewalls and caches living there.

RFC 1958

"The goal is connectivity, the tool is the Internet Protocol, and the intelligence is end to end rather than hidden in the network."

The end-to-end argument

Things like reliable delivery could be built into the network or at the edges. But only Bob can truly check that the right letter arrived intact, so the edges must do it anyway. Doing it inside the network can only be a speed-up.
